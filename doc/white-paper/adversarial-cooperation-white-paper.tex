\begin{abstract}
Adversarial Cooperation seeks solutions to cooperation problems using
cryptographic protocols without a trusted referee, and hardware for their
execution. The aim is cooperation that preserves
private information and the strategic advantages it supports. We seek
resources to develop, analyze, implement, and demonstrate these protocols
and their hardware.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Applied cryptography, cooperation, zero knowledge, multiparty computation,
hardware.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{The Cooperation Problem}
The central question is: \emph{how can adversarial parties cooperate without
a referee while retaining their strategic advantage?} Parties should verify
an agreed result without surrendering cards, strategies, or other secrets.
We are concerned that public and private institutions may underestimate the
information their interactions expose.

The privacy objective is to reveal nothing about private inputs beyond the
agreed outputs and public information. Each protocol must specify its
adversary model, assumptions, and privacy and correctness requirements.

The program builds on commitments, zero-knowledge proofs, and multiparty
computation \cite{naor1991,gmr1989,gmw1987}, alongside prior work on replacing
mediators in strategic games \cite{dodis2000}. No new primitive or general
solution is claimed; each proposed construction requires its own analysis.

\section{Games and Cooperation Problems}
The following cases define initial research targets:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Rock--Paper--Scissors:} Fix each player's choice before either
learns the other's move, then verify the completed round's outcome.
\item \textit{Tic-Tac-Toe:} Demonstrate that a private strategy avoids defeat
under specified game conditions, without disclosing its move-selection rules.
\item \textit{Poker:} Investigate dealing and winner verification without a
trusted dealer, without revealing private cards beyond what the agreed
outcome implies.
\item \textit{Chess:} Determine the outcome between fixed, private deterministic
move-selection algorithms under agreed rules and computation bounds, without
publishing their moves or strategies.
\item \textit{Stag Hunt:} Coordinate mutually beneficial action when each
party's willingness to cooperate depends on the other's participation.
\item \textit{Joint Optimization:} Optimize an agreed objective using private
inputs or constraints, revealing only the agreed result.
\end{itemize}
Each case requires its own specification and analysis. Whether more complex
cooperation can be composed from simpler, elemental games and protocols
remains an open question.

\section{From Specification to Hardware}
The goal is to take formally defined protocols through inspectable executable
representations into specialized hardware that participants can independently
inspect and reproduce. Candidate methods include C reference code, Boolean
circuits, register-transfer-level (RTL) descriptions in Verilog or VHDL, and
FPGA evaluation; choices depend on the protocol.

Translation correctness and correspondence between specification, code,
circuits, and devices are research tasks. Prior work on verified high-level
synthesis provides a foundation \cite{herklotz2021}. Evaluation must address
functional behavior, costs, fabrication and device integrity, and physical
leakage or faults. Functional correspondence alone does not establish
protection against such physical attacks.

\section{Resources and Support}
We seek funding, research collaborators, computing resources, FPGA boards,
and measurement equipment. Support would enable dedicated research,
independent cryptographic review, implementation, and hardware experiments.

For each selected cooperation problem, the aim is a complete, inspectable
demonstrator: protocol definition, implementation, adversarial evaluation,
and hardware realization. Proof status and limitations will be explicit;
a working demonstrator does not establish a cryptographic proof or general
composability.
